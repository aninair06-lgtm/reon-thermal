EBS Cabinet Heat Study
REON EBS Na+ · 30 kW / 60 kWh · 128 sodium-ion cells · MIT Energy & Climate Hack 2026

Bypass fixes hot cells, not a hot cabinet

We simulated REON’s battery cabinet cell by cell to see what makes each cell hot and what switching one cell out of the string can fix. Switching out an aged cell removes its hot spot and gives back up to 7.3 kW on a hot day. Switching out many cells to cool the whole cabinet makes it hotter, because the remaining cells carry more current.

depends on airflow

How uneven are the cells?

Fan on, the middle of the stack runs hottest because the air skips the 30 mm gaps: held at full power the hottest cell (module 7) settles near 61.5 °C in the 3D model (three meshes, ±7%), with cells as cool as 38.2 °C. Sealed, the whole cabinet warms together: 0.6 K spread after 2 h, hottest M08_C04 at the end of discharge.

only for hot spots

Can bypass keep every cell under 60 °C?

On a 40 °C day starting cool: no bypass needed (48.5 °C). When the cabinet starts hot (55 °C): no. Bypassing 13 cells gives 63.5 °C, worse than doing nothing (61.6 °C), because the other cells carry more current. The 3D model agrees: over the 2 h discharge, bypassing 13 cells raises the hottest cell from 48.9 to 51.0 °C.

energy and power

What does it cost?

Bypassing 13 cells at full power adds 11.3% heat, and 30 kW then lasts 1.8 h instead of 2.0 h (52.8 kWh). Bypassing one cell costs 0.8% of power. Up to 4 cells fit inside 30 kW × 2 h with 5% charge headroom.

derate or cool

When is bypass not enough?

When the heat is everywhere. A 55 °C start needs a cut to 27.0 kW (−10%). With one aged cell, a fixed string must drop to 19.5 kW; bypassing just that cell holds 26.8 kW.

The air goes around the cells, not between them

The fan blows 0.15 m³/s in at the bottom front. In our 3D airflow model it takes the easy paths: under the bottom module, up the side gaps and over the top. The seven 30 mm gaps between modules get under 1% of it, so the middle modules sit in still air and run hottest.

Sealed, with no fan, every cell warms together by about 8.8 K over a 2 h discharge (adiabatic would be 9.1 K). The cabinet’s own mass (618 kJ/K) sets the pace, and the spread between cells is only 0.6 K in the 3D model. In both cases the hottest cell is hot because of where it sits, not because of what it is. Bypass cannot fix that.

Only three things beat the limit on their own or in pairs: starting cooler (pre-cool at night), spreading the same energy over more time, and bypassing the aged cell together with pre-cooling or phase-change plates that melt just below the limit. Air conditioning alone does not get there in 2 h: the cool air skips the gaps and the cells hold too much heat. Spreaders, a chimney vent and a reflective roof barely move the hottest cell.

What it is worth

The value of cell-level control is that one weak cell no longer sets the limit for the other 127, for heat or for capacity.

+7.3 kWheld on a hot-start day with one aged cell: 26.8 kW instead of 19.5 kW (14.5 kWh more per hot day)
6.3 → 6.6 yrservice life at a hot site, fixed string vs cell control (to 70%); with one 3×-resistance cell: 5.7 → 6.6 yr
+$1,114net present value per cabinet from the longer life (7% discount, $350/kWh capex)

And for the climate

Each cabinet carries about 3.9 t CO₂ from manufacturing. A longer life spreads that over more years: 0.62 → 0.59 t per year of service, about 0.9 t avoided over 30 years of replacements. Air conditioning sized to protect the worst cell at this site would use about 760 kWh a year (0.28 t CO₂). Control lets that cooling be sized for the average cell, not the worst one.

Air conditioning that cools the whole cabinet would slow ageing more than bypass does. Cell control is not a replacement for cooling a hot site; it removes the outliers that would otherwise force derating or oversizing.

How we got these numbers

  • OpenFOAM 13, cabinet air plus cells: 98,784 cells, anisotropic cells, Boussinesq air; forced case run steady with laminar and k-omega SST; sealed 40 °C case run as a full 2 h transient, with and without the 13 hottest cells bypassed. Internal radiation is not in the 3D model (the network carries it).
  • Elmer FEM 26.2, one module with 16 cells and 15 resolved 0.2 mm interface layers: matches the exact solution to 0.00001 K; three meshes agree within 0.03 K; halving the time step changes the peak by 0.0001 K.
  • A cell-by-cell network model (128 cells, walls, air streams), calibrated to both 3D models, closes energy to under 0.001% in every case. It ran 9,197 scenarios on the GPU for the sensitivity sweep (Sobol), the exact cause split (Shapley) and the bypass sets.
  • All 21 hand checks from the brief reproduce within 1.1%.
  • Left-out check: the network was fitted only to the sealed case without bypass. For the 13-bypassed case it predicts the hottest cell rises by 2.0 K; the 3D model, run separately, gives 2.1 K.
  • Halving the fan (0.075 m³/s, 3D, full power held): hottest cell 72.0 °C vs 67.6 °C on the same mesh (+4.4 K); the average cell rises 7.4 K.
  • OpenFOAM grid-convergence study, three meshes (306,432, 98,784, 25,916 cells): the fan-on hottest cell reads 72.6, 67.6 and 65.2 °C, converging (order 1.3) to 61.5 °C. The fine mesh is still 2.4 K from the medium one, so it misses the brief’s 0.5 K target; grid uncertainty ±7%. The hottest module and the starved gaps are the same on all three.

What we assumed (REON has not published these)

  • Cabinet geometry, inlet and outlet sizes, 1.5 mm steel walls; an all-series string of 128 cells (3.1 V, 151 Ah); 0.25 mΩ bypass switch.
  • Ageing speeds up 1.6× per +10 K (35 kJ/mol), about 7,500 cycles to 70% at 25 °C; $350/kWh, 0.37 kg CO₂/kWh grid.
  • Ranges, not probabilities: peak cell 49.9–62.2 °C across the sampled inputs on a hot-start day. The airflow model choice alone moves the fan-on hot spot by 23.3 K.
  • The cell heat (6.1 W, from the brief) is the assumption that could overturn everything. It means losing 2.6% of the power as heat. The only open sodium-ion model (PyBaMM, Chayambuka 2022, a lab cell, not REON’s) loses 11.3% at the same rate, about 28 W per cell at REON’s size. At that heat a normal 40 °C day peaks at 79.3 °C, and staying under 60 °C would need a cut to 21.3 kW.
  • Models agreeing with each other is not validation. A measured cell heat rate comes first, then thermocouples on cells M06–M07 and gap air speed.
